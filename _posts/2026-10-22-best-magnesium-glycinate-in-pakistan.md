## What Is the Difference Between Magnesium Glycinate and Magnesium Bisglycinate?

There is no difference. Both names describe magnesium bound to glycine, and "bisglycinate" is the more precise term because one magnesium is attached to two glycine molecules (My Vitamin Store product FAQ). So "magnesium bisglycinate capsules" and "magnesium glycinate tablets" are the same ingredient in different formats.

## What Is Elemental Magnesium, and Why Does It Matter on a Supplement Label?

Elemental magnesium is the actual magnesium you receive, and the Supplement Facts panel is supposed to declare it, not the weight of the whole compound (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements). Think of the compound as a parcel. The parcel weighs 850 mg, but the magnesium inside is only a part of it, and the rest is glycine.

Nutrifactor's label shows this clearly: each tablet has 850 mg magnesium glycinate, which equals only 120 mg elemental magnesium (Nutrifactor Magnicell page). That is roughly 14% magnesium by weight.

This is why Herbiotics' "1,000 mg" headline can mislead you. Its product page lists 1,000 mg magnesium glycinate per tablet but does not state the elemental amount (Herbiotics product page). If it uses the same grade as Nutrifactor, that is roughly 140 mg elemental. That is our estimate, not a label claim, so confirm it with the brand before relying on it.

## Why Do Magnesium Glycinate Doses Look So Different Across Brands in Pakistan?

Three things explain it.

### 1. Compound weight vs elemental weight

A label number can describe the whole compound or only the magnesium inside it, and the two are very far apart. Because glycinate is only about 14% magnesium, every 100 mg of glycinate carries roughly 14 mg of magnesium. Here is how that plays out on three real labels:

| What the front of the pack says | What that number measures | Elemental magnesium you actually get |
|---|---|---|
| "850 mg magnesium glycinate" (Nutrifactor) | Weight of the compound | 120 mg (stated on the label) |
| "1,000 mg magnesium glycinate" (Herbiotics) | Weight of the compound | About 140 mg (our estimate, not stated) |
| "250 mg elemental magnesium" (Route2Health) | The magnesium itself | 250 mg (stated on the label) |

The tablet with the smallest number on the front delivers the most magnesium. It also shows why you can't compare 250 mg with 1,000 mg directly.

### 2. Added magnesium oxide

Oxide packs more elemental magnesium into a small tablet. Reaching 250 mg from glycinate alone would take roughly 1,770 mg of compound (250 ÷ 0.14), which is a very large tablet. Route2Health solves this by adding 149.577 mg of oxide to its 850 mg of glycinate. The trade-off is side effects. NIH lists oxide among the magnesium forms most commonly reported to cause diarrhea, and high supplemental doses of any form can cause diarrhea, nausea and abdominal cramping (NIH ODS). In practice, watch for loose stools, nausea or cramps in the first few days, take the tablet with food, and ask a pharmacist about a lower dose or a glycinate-only product if they appear.

### 3. Serving size

Nutrifactor's directions say 1 to 2 tablets daily, so its dose is 120 mg or 240 mg depending on how many you take. Someone taking two tablets gets double the magnesium and pays double per day, so always compare daily dose, not tablet strength.

## Why Is the Rs. 1,250 Bottle Not Actually the Cheaper Magnesium?

Because the bottle price only tells you what leaves your wallet. The cost per mg tells you what you get for it. Here it is in two steps:

1. **Count the magnesium in each bottle.** Route2Health: 250 mg × 30 tablets = 7,500 mg. Nutrifactor: 120 mg × 30 tablets = 3,600 mg.
2. **Divide the price by that total.** Route2Health: Rs. 1,500 ÷ 7,500 mg = Rs. 0.20 per mg. Nutrifactor: Rs. 1,250 ÷ 3,600 mg = Rs. 0.35 per mg.

Put another way, each rupee buys about 5 mg of elemental magnesium from Route2Health and about 2.9 mg from Nutrifactor's 30-tablet bottle. Rs. 250 more gets you about 3,900 mg more magnesium. One honest caveat: cost per mg compares quantity, not form. Route2Health's blend includes oxide and Nutrifactor's does not, so weigh your stomach tolerance alongside the price.

## Why Do Some Magnesium Glycinate Supplements Contain Magnesium Oxide?

Route2Health and Webber Naturals both list oxide alongside glycinate. Webber's label calls the blend "buffered", and Route2Health lists oxide as a separate ingredient. The likely reason is that oxide raises the elemental dose without making the tablet huge.

The trade-off is absorption. NIH explains that forms which dissolve well in liquid are absorbed more completely, and small studies found citrate, lactate, aspartate and chloride were better absorbed than oxide. NIH does not rank glycinate directly in that comparison, so keep that in mind (NIH ODS).

A blend is not automatically bad. It is just not the same as a glycinate-only product, and the page should say so clearly. If you have a sensitive stomach, take it with food and choose a glycinate-only label where you can.

## What Is the Best Time to Take Magnesium Glycinate?

There is no single correct time, and brands disagree because each is aiming at a different goal:

- **Route2Health:** one tablet daily, preferably before a meal (product page).
- **Nutrifactor and Herbiotics:** with a meal. Nutrifactor's FAQ also suggests evening or bedtime (Nutrifactor).
- **Webber Naturals:** with food, and in the evening if you are using it for sleep (My Vitamin Store).

Treat clock time as a routine choice, not a strict rule. Follow your label, take it consistently, and choose a time you will actually remember. Taking it with food is a sensible default if your stomach is sensitive.

Medicines can change the timing, though. NIH says tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics (like doxycycline and ciprofloxacin) should be taken at least 2 hours before or 4 to 6 hours after magnesium. Oral bisphosphonates should be separated by at least 2 hours (NIH ODS). Route2Health also suggests spacing magnesium from calcium or iron (product FAQ).

## Why Do Some People Feel Results Early While Others Feel Nothing?

**Reasons you might feel nothing:**

- **Your dose may be lower than you think.** Nutrifactor's single tablet has 120 mg elemental. A 1,000 mg headline with no elemental number tells you very little.
- **Magnesium may not be your problem.** NIH says symptomatic deficiency from low intake is uncommon in healthy people, and blood tests do not reflect total body magnesium well (NIH ODS). Poor sleep can come from caffeine, stress, screens, pain or other medical causes.
- **Expectations matter.** In a 2025 sleep trial, even the placebo group improved.
- **Brands promise very different timelines.** Herbiotics says results within one week, while Nutrifactor says two to three months (Herbiotics, Nutrifactor). These are marketing statements, not research findings.
- **Competing minerals.** NIH notes that very high zinc doses (142 mg a day) can interfere with magnesium absorption (NIH ODS).

**Reasons you might feel it early:**

- **You may actually have been running low.** NIH lists people with gut conditions, type 2 diabetes, alcohol dependence and older adults as more likely to have inadequate magnesium (NIH ODS).
- **Your main issue may be cramps or tightness,** which people tend to notice faster.
- **You took it consistently and with realistic expectations.**

One more caution: reviews are not evidence. One Herbiotics reviewer credits the tablet with helping them quit smoking. That is a personal story, and nothing on the page backs it up.

## Does Magnesium Glycinate Help You Sleep?

It might, modestly. A 2025 randomized, placebo-controlled trial gave 155 adults with poor sleep either 250 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate or a placebo for four weeks. Insomnia Severity Index scores improved by 3.9 points in the magnesium group and 2.3 points in the placebo group (p = 0.049), and the authors called the improvement modest (trial in *Nature and Science of Sleep*).

So the honest takeaway is a small benefit in a four-week study, with a big placebo response. It is not a cure for insomnia. If poor sleep continues, speak to a doctor.

## Is Magnesium Glycinate Safe? Who Should Check With a Doctor First?

- **Daily limits.** The RDA is 310 to 320 mg for adult women and 400 to 420 mg for adult men, and that counts food too. The upper limit for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg a day, which covers supplements and medicines but not food (NIH ODS).
- **Don't stack.** Count magnesium from multivitamins, antacids and laxatives as well.
- **Side effects.** High supplemental doses can cause diarrhea, nausea and cramping (NIH ODS).
- **Kidney problems.** NIH says the risk of magnesium toxicity rises with impaired kidney function (NIH ODS). Don't self-prescribe.
- **Ask a doctor or pharmacist first** if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have a medical condition or take regular medicines. Route2Health, Nutrifactor and Herbiotics all say this on their pages.
